What the Board is made of

The agency has two halves that do not answer to each other. One decides cases. The other decides which cases get brought. Knowing which is which explains most of what looks strange about how a charge moves.

The two halves

The Board. Five seats in Washington. It decides cases that have already been through a region and a hearing. Members are appointed by the President with the Senate's consent, for five year terms. It can sit in panels of three.

The General Counsel. A separate appointment, for four years. Decides which charges become complaints, and prosecutes them. The Board has no say in that decision and cannot overrule it.

Who has held it

Under the General Counsel sit the 26 regional offices, which take the filings, investigate, and run elections. Hearings are held by administrative law judges. A case only reaches the five members in Washington if somebody files exceptions to a judge's decision, which most cases never do.

This split is why the Board's published decisions are a poor picture of what the agency does. They are the small share of cases that were litigated the whole way. Everything else was settled, withdrawn, or dismissed by a region.
